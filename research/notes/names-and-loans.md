# Names and loans: core–periphery phonology, adaptation, name marking

Scope: should adapted names get a looser "name phonology" (extra final consonants), or stay in the core (C)V(n) phonology with an echo vowel? What evidence bears on recognisability and on name/word collisions? **Extended 2026-11-19 (§5–7):** do Apupiwe's loan rules (L4 panel, median form, fixed adaptation, free borrowing N4) match how real loans arise, compete and change over generations?

## 1. Core–periphery lexicons: do they exist, and are they stable?

- **Japanese.** Itô & Mester describe nested strata: Yamato (native) obeys all three voicing constraints studied, Sino-Japanese is exempt from some, and "the Foreign vocabulary is exempt from all three." [primary] [Itô & Mester 2009, Oxford Handbook of Japanese Linguistics ch. 4](https://people.ucsc.edu/~mester/papers/2009_ito_mester_lexical_classes.pdf). The strata are synchronic, not just history: some old loans (*karuta*, *kappa*) and even a recent one (*sukii* → *take-zukii*) now undergo native compound voicing, so they have "changed stratal membership". Nativization is "a gradual affair". [primary, same]
- **The periphery can grow.** Before about 1890, Japanese palatalised /ti, di/ in loans (*chiketto*, *rajio*). After 1890, /ti/ and /di/ "became allowable sequences in loanwords". [primary, abstract] [Crawford 2009, Cornell PhD](https://ecommons.cornell.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/90cc8c5f-e4bd-4be9-9263-6dc4e217da7b/content). English /v/ became a phoneme partly through French loans with initial /v/ (*fine*/*vine*). [secondary] [Wikipedia: Middle English phonology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_English_phonology)
- **But syllable structure barely moved.** Itô & Mester: across 150 years of Japanese loans, "the syllable structure restrictions have not changed". Only the repair changed, from deletion (19th-c. *fai* "five") to epenthesis (*faibu*). [primary, Itô & Mester 2009]. Even Japan's official two-tier spelling standard has a looser Table 2, for writing foreign words and names "as close as possible to the original sound", and it adds only segments (*ye*, *wi*, *v-*), no final consonants. Proper names (people, companies, products) are exempt from the standard altogether. [primary] [Cabinet Notice 2, 1991, 外来語の表記](https://www.bunka.go.jp/kokugo_nihongo/sisaku/joho/joho/kijun/naikaku/gairai/pdf/94380801_03.pdf)
- **Finnish.** /b, g, f/ occur "only in relatively new loanwords" and are mostly pronounced *p, k, v/hv*. A clearly voiced *b* appears "typically only in educated public speech". New loans get "an *i* after a final consonant" (*bussi*), even though Finnish allows some final consonants. [secondary, reference handbook] [Korpela, Handbook of Finnish: letters](https://jkorpela.fi/finnish/Pronunciation_of_letters.html); [loanwords](https://jkorpela.fi/finnish/Native_words,_loanwords,.html)
- **Hawaiian.** Some loans were adapted. Others "motivated … a division in its lexicon between native, core words and peripheral, foreign ones" (Parker Jones 2018). *Kristo* "Christ" keeps its cluster but still ends in a vowel. [secondary] [Wikipedia: Hawaiian phonology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawaiian_phonology); [Hieber 2025](https://linguisticdiscovery.com/posts/hawaiian-loanwords/). The missionary alphabet used extra letters for foreign and Biblical words. [secondary] [Wikipedia: Hawaiian alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawaiian_alphabet)
- **Who keeps peripheral sounds:** bilinguals. Monolingual chiShona speakers nativize English loans, while bilinguals import /l/ and clusters (Kadenge & Mudzingwa 2012). The rate of importation tracks bilingualism (Haugen 1950). [secondary] [Smith, Loanword Phonology, Cambridge Handbook of Phonology 2e draft](https://users.castle.unc.edu/~jlsmith/home/pdf/smith2024_CHoP2_LoanwordPhonology_circulate.pdf)

**Summary (inferred):** peripheral *segments* can survive and even spread, mostly where speakers are bilingual or where many loans carry them. Peripheral *codas* are the most resisted part, even in languages with deliberately loose foreign tiers. Casual and monolingual speech nativizes toward the core.

## 2. Loanword adaptation: perception vs phonology; repairs

- **Perception account.** "All loanword adaptations are phonetically minimal transformations that apply during speech perception". Japanese listeners hear an illusory vowel inside clusters (Dupoux et al. 1999), so the epenthesis already happens in perception. [primary] [Peperkamp 2005, BLS 30:341](https://journals.linguisticsociety.org/proceedings/index.php/BLS/article/download/919/701/893)
- **Phonology account and mixed views.** Paradis & LaCharité's Preservation Principle says to keep input material unless that is too costly, so epenthesis should beat deletion. Kang's review finds that perception "underdetermines adaptation": Mainland Mandarin *Friedman* → *fu.li.te.man* but Taiwanese *fu.li.man*. Orthography and bilingualism also matter. [primary] [Kang 2011, Blackwell Companion to Phonology ch. 95](https://www.yoonjungkang.com/uploads/1/1/6/2/11625099/tbc_100.kang.pdf)
- **Clusters vs codas.** Word-initial clusters are repaired "predominantly" by epenthesis. For final codas, "it is not clear whether epenthesis is cross-linguistically the preferred strategy". Japanese, Korean and Sesotho insert a vowel. Vietnamese, Thai, Burmese and White Hmong delete, and all of the deleters "have a strong preference for monosyllabic morphemes". Hawaiian and Fijian mix the two (*east* → *isi*). [primary, Kang 2011]
- **Which epenthetic vowel.** Languages use a default vowel (Japanese *u*, Finnish *-i*), copy a neighbouring vowel, or take the vowel's quality from the consonant. Uffmann found all three "frequently found" in Shona, Sranan, Samoan and Kinyarwanda corpora. [abstract] [Uffmann 2006, Lingua 116](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0024384105001051). Even Japanese's default vowel is coloured by coarticulation in the cluster. [primary, abstract] [Guevara-Rukoz et al. 2017, JASA-EL](http://www.lscp.net/persons/peperkamp/Guevara-Rukoz_Lin_Morii_Minagawa_Dupoux_Peperkamp_(2017)_Which_epenthetic_vowel._Phonetic_categories_versus_acoustic_detail_in_perceptual_vowel_epenthesis.pdf)
- **Recognisability: epenthesis vs deletion.** Itô & Mester: deletion forms keep the source's syllable count, but "the phonemic (and orthographic) form of the source word is hard to recover". Epenthesis forms keep all consonants, so the source is "largely recoverable". [primary, scholarly judgement, not an experiment]. This fits Cutler et al. 2000: when listeners repair a nonword they change vowels faster and more accurately than consonants, because vowels "constrain lexical selection less tightly". [primary] [Memory & Cognition 28:746](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/BF03198409.pdf)
- **Copy vs default vowel:** I found no recognisability study that compares them. **Gap.**

## 3. Proper-name marking

- **Personal articles exist in unrelated families:** Tagalog *si/ni/kay* (by case) [secondary] [tagaloglang](https://www.tagaloglang.com/si/); Māori *a* (*a Pita*) and Catalan *en/na*, *el/la* [secondary] [Wikipedia: Article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Article_(grammar)); Fijian proper article *o* [secondary] [Swarthmore LING073, after Dixon 1988](https://wikis.swarthmore.edu/ling073/Fijian/Grammar). The Catalan article grew out of Latin *dominus/domina* and lost its honorific meaning. [abstract] [OUP chapter](https://academic.oup.com/book/36485/chapter/321157672)
- **Names are phonologically special.** Names resist alternations through "name-specific faithfulness". They can have larger inventories, e.g. English [gwɛ] only in *Gwen*. [abstract/snippet] [Tanaka 2023, Lang. & Ling. Compass](https://compass.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/lnc3.12502) (page 403). Learners "are generally willing to accept idiosyncratic patterns … in proper nouns", but they extend only natural patterns to *new* names. [primary] [Tanaka 2023, Phonology, final draft](https://www2.ninjal.ac.jp/yutanaka/papers_cv/Tanaka2023_Learning_biases_in_proper_nouns.pdf)
- **Do listeners use name marking?** Two-year-olds read "This is Zav" (no article) as a proper name for familiar kinds of referent (Katz, Baker & Macnamara 1974; Hall 1991). [secondary, snippet] [Hall 1991, Child Dev.](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1467-8624.1991.tb01595.x). A name that is also a word stays hard to learn *as a name*: Baker the surname is recalled worse than baker the job. [abstract/secondary] [McWeeny et al. 1987, BJP 78:143](https://bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.2044-8295.1987.tb02235.x). I found no study of whether an obligatory name marker speeds adult comprehension. **Gap.**

## 4. Recognisability of heavily adapted names

- Direct evidence is thin. A pilot study found that Japanese learners could spell and translate English words that have Japanese loanword counterparts at "more than twice the rate" of words without them, so knowing the adapted form helps people recover the source. [primary] [Delve & Broderick 2022, JALT](https://jalt-publications.org/sites/default/files/pdf-article/jalt2021-pcp-022.pdf). I found no experiment on English speakers recovering *Kalikimaka*-type forms by ear. **Gap; UNVERIFIED** that they mostly can't.

## 5. After adoption: how loans change over generations

- **Older loans are more nativized, within a language.** WOLD codes each clearly borrowed word as highly integrated, intermediate or unintegrated, and dates it. Comparing layers *within* one language (coders' scales differ between languages), the older layer is more often "highly integrated": Hausa pre-modern Islamic 78% vs modern 37%; Malagasy pre-migration 88% vs 19th c. or later 5%; Yaqui colonial 73% vs modern 15%; Indonesian Early Malay 52% vs modern 35%; Kali'na 17th c. 100% vs 20th c. 62% [computed, my count from the WOLD CLDF export, [lexibank/wold](https://github.com/lexibank/wold), 2026-11-19]. Japanese has no "highly integrated" loans at all, and its modern layer is 54% unintegrated vs 0% for Old Japanese [computed, same]. Caveat: older layers also differ in source language and in which words survived. Itô & Mester's "changed stratal membership" (§1) is the same process seen word by word.
- **New layers come as doublets, not replacements.** Japanese kept *koppu* (Dutch/Portuguese, 17th c.) beside *kappu* (English) with split meanings, and *garasu* "glass (material)" beside *gurasu* "drinking glass"; other doublets are synonyms. [secondary] [Wikipedia: Gairaigo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gairaigo) (page flagged as needing citations). Indonesian *telepon* (Dutch *telefoon*, f → p) has the recorded variants *télpon, telpon, télefon, telfon*: a reduced form and a form with the source's *f* side by side. [primary] [WOLD: Indonesian "the telephone"](https://wold.clld.org/word/71261839678273614). Many Arabic loans in Indonesian have undergone "re-Arabization". [abstract/secondary, search snippet only] [Ijaz Arabi journal](https://ejournal.uin-malang.ac.id/index.php/ijazarabi/article/download/21090/10578)
- **Re-adaptation by bilinguals is partial and ordered.** English loans that reached Korean through Japanese (*cup*: Japanese *kappu*, classic Korean *kat.p\*u*; direct adaptation today *khəp*) were "partially updated to conform to the direct English-Korean correspondences ... while also retaining some Japanese traits". Laryngeal features were most likely to be updated, "followed by epenthetic vowels and liquids; nonepenthetic vowels were most resistant": variants *khap\*u, khaphu, khap.phi, khaphi*, never schwa. [primary, abstract] [Kenstowicz 2012, NINJAL abstract, summarising Kang, Kenstowicz & Ito 2008, JEAL 17](https://www2.ninjal.ac.jp/past-events/jk2012/Kenstowicz%20abstract.pdf). Whole replacement also happens (*semento* → *shiment'u* "cement"). [abstract/secondary, snippet] [Tranter 1997, Linguistics 35](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/91243/)
- **Who pulls a loan back toward its source:** speakers proficient in the source, or talking to people who are (Lev-Ari et al. 2014: the interlocutors' bilingualism mattered, the speaker's own did not); speakers with favourable attitudes or a "globalist" identity (Jaggers 2018). The reverse also happens: Acehnese bilinguals "overadapt" Indonesian loans to sound more Acehnese. [primary] [Smith 2024, §3.3](https://users.castle.unc.edu/~jlsmith/home/pdf/smith2024_CHoP2_LoanwordPhonology_circulate.pdf)
- **Communities fix conventions, even against perception.** Korean adaptation of Japanese, English and French is systematic but diverges from listeners' perception; the authors attribute it to "social conventions, including official prescriptive standards"; De Jong & Cho (2012) to an "explicit sociocultural standard". [primary, Smith 2024 §3.3.3]
- **Long loans get clipped.** Japanese clips back (*čokoreeto* → *čoko*), front (*puraQtohoomu* → *hoomu*), middle (*eNtaateemeNto* → *eNtame*) and compounds (*waapuro*). [secondary, review of Irwin 2011] [LINGUIST List 24.1739](https://listserv.linguistlist.org/pipermail/linguist/2013-April/063267.html). Clipped compounds are usually two or four morae (*paasonaru konpyuutaa* → *pasokon*). [secondary, poster] [GLOW in Asia XII](https://glowlinguistics.org/asia12/wp-content/uploads/sites/7/2019/07/Poster9-8.pdf). Indonesian *telpon* (above); Icelandic *bíll* from "automobile" "via the Danish shortened version". [secondary] [Wikipedia: Linguistic purism in Icelandic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistic_purism_in_Icelandic). I found no cross-linguistic rate of clipping by loan length. **Gap.**
- **Established loans keep spreading.** NINJAL's national surveys: answering "use the loan" for ゲット over 獲得 rose 14.0% (2000) → 38.0% (2016), スーツ over 背広 52.8% → 68.2%; オープン/開店 and トータル/合計 barely moved. Among friends, サポート displaced native 手助け as the majority choice between 2004 and 2023. Loans win in casual speech; native words hold more of formal writing. This happened during and after NINJAL's own 2002–06 proposals to paraphrase hard loans. [primary] [NINJAL 2024, 外来語使用実態調査 overview, Table 3 and Fig. 11](https://repository.ninjal.ac.jp/record/2000287/files/lppj_loanwordsurvey_202302_overview_202408.pdf)

**Summary (inferred):** a loan's consonant skeleton is stable for centuries (*koppu*). What moves is the periphery: epenthetic vowels, liquids, laryngeal features, length. Bilinguals and prestige pull it toward the source, monolinguals and time pull it toward the core. The usual result is variants and doublets, not one form replacing another.

## 6. Where loans come from, and who wins when planned and borrowed forms compete

- **One immediate source is the norm.** Of 12,684 clearly borrowed WOLD words with a recorded source, 96.2% have one immediate source language; 3.8% (480) have several. The multi-source cases are mostly neighbours whose forms already agree: French + Latin (51), Javanese + Sundanese (49), Bulgarian + Serbian (40), Czech + Slovak (26), Arabic + Hindi-Urdu + Persian (22). 21.6% also record an earlier, non-immediate source, i.e. a chain. [computed, WOLD CLDF]. So multi-source borrowing exists where the candidate sources share a form, which is the L4 condition. But I found **no natural language or academy that sets a form as the median of many languages' pronunciations.** **Gap / no precedent found.**
- **Via a dominant language.** Korean took English via Japanese (above); Indonesian took French *téléphone* via Dutch *telefoon* (WOLD, above). Swahili's loans, 1800–2000: "mainly loans from English". [primary] [WOLD Swahili chapter, Schadeberg](https://wold.clld.org/vocabulary/1). In India, translation from German, French and Russian "has mostly been carried out via English". [primary] [Ray 1982, Translating and the Computer 4](https://aclanthology.org/1982.tc-1.17.pdf)
- **Academies name one source, usually English, and keep what's current.** Indonesian (PUPI, 3rd ed. 2005): foreign languages only when Indonesian and related languages have no fit term; then "sumber rujukan yang diutamakan ialah istilah Inggris yang pemakaiannya bersifat internasional", borrowed "dengan mengutamakan bentuk visualnya" (spelling first), and preferred when shorter than the translation or already known. [primary] [Pedoman Umum Pembentukan Istilah, Wikisource](https://id.wikisource.org/wiki/Pedoman_Umum_Pembentukan_Istilah). India's 1960 CSTT rules: "International terms should be retained as such", transliterated "at maximum approximation of source-language pronunciation"; loans from English, Portuguese, French or Arabic "which have gained currency ... should be retained". [primary, as quoted by Ray 1982; the CSTT site refused connection]
- **Swahili: speakers keep the loan that arrived first.** Among 50 Tanzanian university staff and students, 64% of 40 BAKITA-era ICT coinages were not what they use (range 57.5–67.5% by group). Unfamiliar coinages included *sanidi* "install" and *kiwaa* "monitor"; accepted ones include *barua pepe* "e-mail", *pakua* "download", *tovuti*, *runinga* "television". The reasons given: no community involvement, too little promotion, and slowness: "most of the formed terms are introduced when we have already adopted the English terms". [primary, small sample] [Mziray 2019, IJLLT 2(6)](https://al-kindipublisher.com/index.php/ijllt/article/view/633)
- **Turkey: planning beat a fading source, not the international one.** The Turkic share of words in five newspapers rose from 35% (1931) to 60.5% (1965); Arabic fell from 51% to 26% (İmer 1973). Today 66% of the 1,000 most frequent written words are Turkic. Yet 77 (7.7%) are European internationalisms, which survived the most forceful purism on record: *telefon, televizyon, internet, otobüs, sistem, program*. [primary] [Yalçınkaya 2026, DTCF Dergisi 66(1)](https://dergipark.org.tr/tr/download/article-file/5190964)
- **Hebrew: an active Academy, mixed uptake.** Some coinages catch on (*z'lolet* "junk food" is offered as a candidate). Others don't: *nechdan* failed as "nephew" and was reassigned; speakers say *date*, not the Academy's *p'gisha romantit*; *peersing* and *boonker* are what's said. The Academy "does not believe that every foreign word should be replaced". [secondary, journalism] [Tablet, "Embrace the Ivrit"](https://www.tabletmag.com/sections/news/articles/embrace-the-ivrit). Its *mirshetet* (2002) for "internet": no usage data found. **Gap.**
- **Icelandic: purism holds where attitudes back it.** Among the Nordic nations, Icelanders had the most negative attitudes to English borrowings. Their newspapers had 17 borrowings per 10,000 running words, against 111 in Norwegian newspapers. In a matched-guise test (361 participants), the guise with neologisms (*tölvupóstur* "e-mail") beat the one with *í-meil* on all eight traits. But people under 30 were "less negative" toward English borrowings, and purism was strongest among the highly educated. [primary, reviewing Kristiansen & Vikør 2006] [Kristinsson & Hilmarsson-Dunn, in *Language Destandardisation in Late Modern Europe*](https://lanchart.hum.ku.dk/research/slice/publications-and-news-letters/publications/language-destandardisation-in-late-modern-europe/p._331-354_Kristinsson_and_Hilmarsson-Dunn.pdf). **French:** the corpus comparison of *courriel* vs *e-mail* in Quebec and France was behind a bot wall. **Gap.**

**Summary (inferred):** a planned form wins when it is there first, is short and transparent, and is backed by schooling and media, or when it replaces a source whose prestige is fading (Arabic and Persian in Turkey), or when the community's attitudes strongly back purism (Iceland). It loses in casual speech when a loan from a shared prestige language is already in use (Swahili ICT, Japanese, Hebrew, Tok Pisin in `auxlangs.md` §1.8).

## 7. Designed and revived languages in practice

- **Esperanto.** For "computer", early *komputero* and *komputoro* "were eventually replaced by the internal creation *komputilo*". Several dozen root neologisms for *mal-* antonyms were proposed; "few have met with much acceptance". Europeans tend to favour international roots, Asians to replace them (that last claim is flagged "citation needed"). [secondary] [Wikipedia: Modern evolution of Esperanto, citing Piron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_evolution_of_Esperanto). Esperanto speakers are "dis-inclined to incorporate other-language material", and coinages from Esperanto morphemes are preferred to borrowings. [abstract/secondary, snippet; full text walled] [Fiedler, in Fiedler & Brosch 2022, *Esperanto: Lingua Franca and Language Community*](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1075/wlp.10.c22/pdf). Native speakers: proper nouns resist *-o* and Esperanto stress (Bergen 2001, `designed-languages-going-native.md` §1). No study of native speakers' lexical borrowing found. **Gap.**
- **Interlingua: the nearest designed precedent to a "world form".** A word is international "when its presence is attested in at least three of the language units, Italian, Spanish and/or Portuguese, French and English, with German and Russian as possible substitutes". Its form is the prototype, "the nearest common documented or hypothetical ancestor from which all contributing variants can be developed", so the prototypes "are neither English nor German, not Latin or Greek". [primary] [Gode 1951, Interlingua-English Dictionary, Introduction](https://interlingua.com/ied/intro). So a threshold over a panel plus a form that belongs to no single member has precedent, though Interlingua's form is an ancestor, not a median. No data found on whether users kept its prototypes. **Gap.**
- **Modern Hebrew:** the sounds followed the founders, not the planners (`designed-languages-going-native.md` §2). In vocabulary, planners and speakers both use phono-semantic matching, a native root picked to sound like the international word (*miškafayim* "glasses", from Hebrew √šqp and Greek *skopéō*). [secondary] [Wikipedia: Phono-semantic matching, after Zuckermann 2003](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phono-semantic_matching). Loans still win much of casual speech (§6).
- **Globasa** changed its own forms in batched phases chosen by its designers (`globasa.md`). I found no case of speakers' borrowings displacing its forms, but it has no native speakers. **Tok Pisin**, the one control with native speakers: English loans displaced transparent phrases (*haus mani* → *bank*) (`auxlangs.md` §1.8).

**Summary (inferred):** in designed languages, internal derivations beat early international roots when the community is international and has no shared prestige language (Esperanto). Where one prestige language is shared (Hebrew and Tok Pisin with English), speakers' loans win in casual use.

## Implications for the design

1. **The motivating collision isn't a coda problem.** Mara → *mala* collides because of r → l. It has no final consonant, so a name phonology with extra codas would not fix it. Collisions are structural: the lexicon fills 210 of 4,550 possible (C)VCV shapes (4.6%), so about **46 of every 1,000** disyllabic names would land on a word [computed]. Only marking catches all of them.
2. **Name marking: recommend a personal article** (one CV grammar word before every name, like *si*/*a*/*o*). It has precedent in Austronesian and Romance, costs one syllable, and children use article cues to spot names. It also makes a collision harmless in the way English copes with *Rose*, *Bill* and *Baker* [inferred].
3. **Name codas: propose not adding them** [inferred from §1]. Even Japan's loosest tier never admitted codas. Finnish adds *-i* to new loans and still nativizes loan segments in casual speech. Importation depends on bilingualism, which most native speakers won't have. The likely result is doublets (a coda form beside an echo-vowel form) plus leakage into the core through brand names that become ordinary words. *Add-back trigger:* tests show marked names are still often confused with each other or with words, in ways a final consonant would fix.
4. **Epenthetic vowel: keep echo/copy, or switch to a fixed default; neither is shown better.** Copy is the most common pattern in Uffmann's corpora. A default (Finnish *-i*) is predictable, so listeners can strip it. Collision risk is the same either way: the lexicon's final vowels and same-vowel CVCV shapes are both at chance, about 20% each [computed]. Whichever you choose, **insert a vowel; never delete a consonant**, because consonants carry word identity (Cutler 2000; Itô & Mester 2009).

*Added 2026-11-19, from §5–7. All inferred from the evidence cited there; none of it is decided.*

5. **Keep fixed adaptation rules; they are what real communities converge on.** Korean adaptation follows conventions and official standards even against perception (§5), and academies publish rules (PUPI, CSTT, Japan 1991). Fixed rules applied by a tool match that.
6. **But expect each loan's periphery to vary and its consonants to stay.** Bilingual speakers update laryngeal features, epenthetic vowels and liquids first, and keep the base vowels (Korean). In Apupiwe those are the final **-u** and **l** from r. A speaker fluent in the source will say *konpyuter*-like variants; a monolingual will say **konpuyutalu**. Rule 11.3 already allows "its own sounds or the language's" (B9), so nothing needs to change. *Option for the author:* state in 11.3 that a loan's identity is its consonants and that its added vowels and **l** may be said as in the source. *Watch (computable):* no two loans, and no loan and root, should differ only in an added vowel or in an **l** that came from r, since those are the positions speakers will vary.
7. **The "world median" form has no precedent in natural languages, but no evidence against it either.** 96% of loans have one immediate source, and the multi-source ones are where sources already agree (§6), which is what L4 requires. The closest designed precedent is Interlingua: a panel threshold plus a form that belongs to no single member. Two differences from real practice to keep in mind. (a) Academies anchor on English and often on its spelling (PUPI), while Apupiwe uses the panel's pronunciation. That is more neutral, but literate bilinguals may pull spellings and pronunciations toward the English or local form (Smith 2024 §3.2). (b) Because so much borrowing goes through English, the panel median will often equal the English-mediated form anyway. Neither calls for a rule change.
8. **Free borrowing (N4) will win in casual speech wherever a native root faces a loan the speaker's other language already uses.** Swahili ICT coinages (64% unused), Japanese (loans gaining for 20 years despite official paraphrases), Hebrew and Tok Pisin all show this. Turkish shows that even the strongest state purism kept internationalisms like *telefon*. The concepts most at risk are those whose loan narrowly *failed* the panel and got a native root (e.g. police, 11/5, slated for a native root in `docs/roadmap.md`). Speakers who say *polis* will be making a doublet, and B9 accepts that. *Watch:* list the panel near-misses and expect doublets there. No change: L4 governs the designed vocabulary only, and §6 shows a native form survives best when it is there first. Apupiwe's seed-then-release (native roots present from the first day) is the condition under which planned forms did best.
9. **Expect clipping of long loans, and check where the clips would land.** 30 of Apupiwe's 82 loans (36.6%) have 4 or more syllables; no non-loan root has more than 3 [computed, `data/lexicon.tsv`]. Japanese, Indonesian and Icelandic all clip such words (§5). Under a naive "first two syllables" clip, 4 of the 30 land on existing roots: **dokutolu** → **doku** "work", **tulakutalu** → **tula** "do", **silukasu** → **silu** "photo", **pasupolutu** → **pasu** "food" [computed; real clipping patterns vary, e.g. Japanese 2+2 compound clips]. This fits the rule of no designed shortening (seed then release). The vocabulary method already prefers a world-shared short form when one passes (*moto*, *TV*). *Watch:* when a clip appears in use, treat a clash like those above as a drift finding (`docs/drift.md`), not as a reason to pre-empt it.
10. **Doublets are the normal outcome, not a failure.** Japanese *koppu*/*kappu*, Indonesian *telepon*/*telefon*, Korean partial updates (§5). If speakers re-borrow a loan closer to its source, the old and new forms will coexist and may split in meaning. Nothing in the rules needs to stop that (B9). The tools only need to keep giving one reference form (11.2).
