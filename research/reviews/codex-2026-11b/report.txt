1. high | B1, B2, B9, P3. “ti wu obu” means “please come” as an instruction. “tiwu obu” can mean “the child is coming.” Likewise, “lo wu obu” is a polite request, while “lowu obu” can report that a man is coming. The consonants and vowels are identical within each pair. Stress carries the difference between directing someone and reporting an event (2.4, 2.6, 4.2.2, 7.1.1, 10.2.1, lexicon.tsv). Reassign “tiwu” and “lowu,” or change the polite construction. Check proposed replacements against whole utterances as well as individual words.

2. high | B2, B10. Several existing grammar words have precisely the vulnerability 2.12 guards against in new words. “wan doku fan ba” means “I work for you,” while “wan doku fun ba” means “I work like you.” After a noun, “yu” asks which and “ye” means some but not all. Each pair occupies the same position and differs only in an unstressed vowel (2.4, 4.4.1, 4.8.3, 5.2.8, 13.1, grammar.tsv). Apply the vowel rule to the existing inventory before teaching it. Test these pairs in complete sentences. Actual confusion rates are unverified.

3. high | B3, B4. Spoken quotation lacks a reliable end. In 3.3 and 4.4.6, quotation marks show whether Mara said both sentences in “I am working. I am eating.” Move the closing mark after “working” and the second “I” can be the narrator. Both versions have the same words and an ordinary sentence boundary in the same place. Give speakers optional words for opening and closing an exact quotation, including one that ends mid-turn.

4. high | B3, B4, B6, P4. The spelling procedure loses information in identifiers. Rules 3.2 and 3.4 allow case-sensitive material such as code and file names to pass through in writing. Under 11.5, the file names “A” and “a” both spell aloud as “alufa.” A listener following the procedure cannot recover which file was named. Add spoken upper- and lowercase markers for literal spelling, plus a numeric character-code fallback for characters outside the spelling alphabet.

5. high | B1, B3, B7. Rule 4.5.4 makes the direction of an action depend on whether its subject “can act.” With “lobotu” (robot) and “alun” (break), a robot can break a cup and can itself break. The suggested “nen” makes the robot act on itself. “gun alun” describes its state under 4.7.4. Neither repair specifically conveys the event of the robot breaking down. Establish a regular change-of-state construction using the existing “lewe” (become), and show the same subject causing, undergoing, and deliberately causing itself to undergo a change.

6. high | B3, B7, L2. The proposed escape for a difficult name fails in one of its recommended positions. Rule 11.1 says a name containing a grammar word ends at “kiyu” and suggests moving it to a topic. The same rule gives an open topic priority over the name when “kiyu” arrives (also 4.3.2). A name such as “Mara Ben,” containing the actual particle “ben,” therefore still lacks its promised closer there. Give explicitly bounded names their own closing convention, usable inside topics and relative clauses, with an escape for the closer itself.

7. medium | B3, B7, B8. One “kiyu” closes every open relative clause (5.3.5). Consider “If a child who sees a dog that bites in the garden calls, …” A speaker who wants the garden to be where the child sees the dog cannot close just “that bites” before adding the location. The suggested second sentence is costly here because the child and dog may exist only within the hypothetical. Let an explicit closer end one relative clause at a time. Keep the existing pause for cases where everything ends together.

8. medium | B2, B9. The accepted loan “geni” (gene) has source pronunciation “[dʒen]” in loans.tsv. Rule 11.3 allows restoration of the source pronunciation where a vowel was added. Under 2.9 the derivation marker “gen” can itself sound like “[dʒen]” (5.4.2, grammar.tsv). This leaves stress or context to distinguish the reduced loan from the marker. Require the final “i” for this loan, or select a form whose permitted reductions stay distinct. Listener confusion is unverified.

9. medium | B3, B6. Rule 12.2 assigns “should” to the advice act “fon,” while 7.1.2 gives a sentence one speech act. “Should I leave?” asks for a judgment. “She should have left” can assess a past obligation. Issuing advice changes what the speaker is doing, and “kifa” supplies the stronger meaning “must.” Establish a predicate for weak obligation that can be questioned, negated, and reported. Keep “fon” for giving advice.

10. medium | B3, B7. Equivalent numbers can give different confidence judgments. Under 6.1.2, “pe” followed by 0.5 means half a chance in six, about 8.3%. With 50/100 it means 50%, even though 9.2.7 defines the latter expression as the fraction one half. The listener has to preserve the denominator to know which scale the speaker used. Use the existing “lonwon” (percent) to mark a percentage explicitly and give unmarked “pe” numbers one scale.

11. medium | B3, B4, B6. Reading arithmetic signs “in the order written” under 9.2.5 supplies the same listed words for “(2 + 3) × 4” and “2 + (3 × 4).” The answers are 20 and 14. The rule supplies no spoken parentheses, and 3.4 leaves other notation to local convention. Add opening and closing group words and show a nested calculation that another speaker can write back exactly.

12. medium | B1, B7. The distributive example in 9.2.6 puts the count on the wrong side of its noun. “wan neke de yefi len ewo mesun” is glossed “I give three to each person,” but 5.1.1 and 5.2.5 put the count after the noun. Here “de” is three and “yefi” is thing (numbers.tsv, lexicon.tsv). Teach “wan neke yefi de len ewo mesun.” This also gives learners an example with an unmistakable boundary between the things given and their recipients.

13. medium | B1, B6, C4. The established scorpion compound says that the animal “bites with its tail” (5.2.3, compounds.tsv). The lexicon confirms “kawa” means bite and “kenlo” means tail. A scorpion stings with its tail. Change the compound’s action to piercing or stinging, so a child learning the animal’s name also learns the correct behavior.

14. medium | B4, B6, L3. Rule 3.4 explicitly promises spoken readings for “20 °C,” and 9.2.6 does the same for “60 km/h.” Rule 9.3.5 gives metric prefixes and several units, but the supplied tables give “yinse” for degree without a Celsius name, and the rate rule gives no complete reading of its own slash example. Specify the readings of these two examples, including the temperature scale and the denominator unit, then use them as patterns for other measurements.

15. medium | B5, L4, N3. The evidence supplied for admitting some loans falls short of the stated borrowing test. For example, loans.tsv gives “suwetalu” (sweater) one source pronunciation, and “ofisu” (office) already has the native alternative “sen pi natun” in 5.4.6. Neither fact establishes how widely the borrowed form itself is shared, as 11.3 and L4 require. Their eligibility is unverified. Record the independent language-family evidence for each marginal official loan. Where that evidence is insufficient, the existing free-borrowing rule lets speakers use the word without fixing it in the core.

16. low | B1, B3, B7. Rule 4.4.1 presents “osa nan tanun tufun” as “tea with lemon.” In lexicon.tsv, “osa” is water, “tanun” fruit, “tufun” sour, and tea has its own root, “tiyen.” The proposed relative-clause repair clarifies attachment but still describes water with sour fruit. Use “tiyen” for tea and gloss the fruit generically, or identify lemon if that species matters. This keeps the example focused on the attachment distinction it is meant to teach.

Verdict

Defer the freeze. The first changes I would make are the command–noun sound collisions, the spoken boundaries for quotations and names, and the rule for who undergoes an action. Each affects ordinary sentences that learners would otherwise have to relearn.
Rationale §§3.2 and 11 report no human listening test. The form collisions above are verifiable from the files. How often listeners miss them, and which replacements work across accents, remains unverified.
