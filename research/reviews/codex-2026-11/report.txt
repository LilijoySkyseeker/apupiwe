1. high — B2, B4. “to nagu tiwu ye” means “I see some of the children.” “to nagu tiwuye” means “I see a spring.” Both have the same sounds and the same first-syllable stress under 2.4 and 2.6. Likewise, “kafe we” is “all the coffee,” while “kafewe” is “tree trunk” (5.2.8, grammar.tsv, lexicon.tsv). Careful pronunciation supplies no required boundary cue. Rename the rarer roots and check proposed roots against ordinary phrases with the same stress.

2. high — B3, B4, B7. The precise name form breaks inside a relative clause. Under 11.1, a name containing a grammar word runs through “kiyu,” but an open relative clause has priority over the name when “kiyu” arrives. A speaker naming an organization with “ya” in its name inside a “gi” clause needs to end the name and continue the clause. The specified closer ends the clause first. Give names a dedicated optional closing word that works at any depth, with an escape for that word inside a name.

3. high — B2, B9. The vocabulary violates the explicit accent safeguard in 2.12. “dundi” (respect) and “lunli” (hell) differ only by d/l in two positions. “wuye” (show) and “guge” (grow) differ only by the listed w/g before u and y/g before e (lexicon.tsv). These are counterexamples to the form-selection rule. Whether speakers actually merge them is unverified. Reassign one member of each pair and check whole words with the listed substitutions allowed wherever the rule permits them.

4. high — B1, B3, P3. Rule 4.5.4 makes the listener decide who underwent an action by deciding whether the subject “can act.” With “lobotu ben alun,” a robot can break something and can itself break. The offered repair “nen” adds self-directed action, which is a different claim from a breakdown with no specified cause (4.7.4, 10.3.6, lexicon.tsv). Give an explicit event form for “X broke” with X as the affected thing. Teach it alongside “X broke it,” “X broke itself,” and the existing state “X is broken.”

5. high — B4, B6, P4. Rule 3.4 preserves case-sensitive code and file names in writing. The cross-cultural spelling procedure in 11.5 gives “A7” and “a7” the same sequence, “alufa oma.” A listener following it cannot reliably reconstruct which file was named. Add a literal-spelling mode with spoken case, space, punctuation, and end markers. Verify it by having a listener reconstruct pairs that differ in exactly one character.

6. high — B1, B3, B6. compounds.tsv assigns exactly “ban kuwi” to both “warehouse” and “shelter.” That matters in a direction such as “go to the shelter.” The two entries even assume different verb senses. One says “keeping,” the other “protects.” lexicon.tsv assigns “protect” to “kuwi” and “keep, retain” to “meko” (5.4.1–5.4.4, 14.2). Keep the protection-based form for shelter and build warehouse from storing goods. Update the “wa kuwi” keeping example in 5.4.3 at the same time.

7. medium — B3, B7, B10. Rule 4.2.6 makes the interpretation of a second verb depend on whether the first takes an object. It includes “teach, see, like, help” in an open-ended list. Rule 4.2.3 lets any content word serve as a noun, and 4.5.1 permits object omission. lexicon.tsv gives learners glosses without the permitted verb patterns. A learner encountering a new verb cannot reliably infer whether the following action is what someone did, what they observed, or how they acted. Give verbs their complement patterns and teach an explicit form that identifies who performs the embedded action.

8. medium — B2, B1. “mon oyi me sen kusa” and “mon oyi me son kusa” report that different people are leaving — the listener in the first, the reported speaker in the second (10.3.4, grammar.tsv). The two unstressed pronouns differ only in their vowel. Rule 2.12 recognizes precisely this risk for new grammar words, while leaving it in the current inventory. Change one of these pronouns before learners acquire it and test the pair in reported speech. Its actual error rate is unverified.

9. medium — B3, B7. Rule 4.4.4 says a final clause attaches to the innermost clause unless a pause closes it, adding that “kiyu” does not. Rule 5.3.5 and Lesson 12 explicitly use “kiyu” to move “pu leso” outside a relative clause. Moreover, 5.3.5 closes every open relative at once. A speaker can return to the main clause but cannot use that word to return just to an outer relative. Specify which clause types it closes and make one occurrence close one level. Give a nested example where the time belongs to the outer relative.

10. medium — B3, B6, P4. Rules 11.1 and 11.3 give incompatible instructions for a borrowed verb that sounds like an existing word. The speaker is told to add “fo” for a collision, but a form marked with “fo” is a name and can never be a verb. This removes the advertised escape exactly when a borrowed action needs it. Give quoted or newly borrowed vocabulary a marker that preserves its grammatical role, and demonstrate a colliding loan in verb position.

11. medium — B3, B6. Rule 12.2 and Lesson 13 translate “should” with the advice particle “fon.” That works for giving advice. Asking “Should I leave?” or reporting “She thought I should have left” requires the recommendation to be the content of a question or thought. “bi fon” is unavailable under the one-act rule in 7.1.2, and “kifa” expresses the stronger “must.” Define a predicate or a conventional paraphrase for “it is advisable that,” and demonstrate it in a question and a past report.

12. medium — B3, B4, B6. Rule 9.2.5 promises an oral reading of arithmetic notation and gives words for the operations. It gives no shared reading for parentheses. In “(2 + 3) × 4” and “2 + 3 × 4,” the listed words and their order are identical if grouping goes unspoken, yet the answers are 20 and 14. Specify spoken opening and closing parentheses and include this pair as the example.

13. medium — B1, B6, B7. Several established computer terms teach obsolete or missing component senses. compounds.tsv calls “apu pi udin” a “word of locking,” although lexicon.tsv distinguishes “apu” (language) from “ila” (word). It gives “tinli la” for keyboard, although “tinli” is specifically a lock key and “la” only marks plurality. Its contacts entry says one contact is “suso,” absent from the supplied word lists (5.2.6, 11.4, 14.2). Rebuild password with “ila,” distinguish the keyboard device from its individual keys, and supply a listed singular for contact.

14. medium — B7, B10. The loan adaptation rule and the published loans teach competing pronunciations. Rule 11.2 maps the sound in English “j” to d. loans.tsv nevertheless maps “[dʒen]” to “geni” and “[teknolodʒi]” to “tekunologi.” Hard g is the reference sound, while the j-like pronunciation before e or i is optional (2.2, 2.9, Lesson 1). State when this source sound yields g, or change the entries to follow the existing rule. Learners should be able to predict the reference form without adopting a particular accent.

15. medium — B3, C2, L2. compounds.tsv gives “mole nun sine,” literally “mother and father,” as the general expression for “parents.” That changes the claim when the parents are two mothers or two fathers. The language already has “iwola” (parent) and optional plural “la” (5.2.6, lexicon.tsv). Use “iwola la” for parents and retain “mole nun sine” for the particular pair it names.

16. medium — B1, B3. Rule 8.2.2 and Lesson 10 gloss “kon nelon ni” both as “a week from now” and “next week.” On a Sunday, the next calendar week can begin tomorrow, while a one-week offset is seven days away. That difference matters when making an appointment. Reserve the offset construction for elapsed time and use the existing “lolo” (next) with “nelon” for the following calendar week.

17. low — B7, B10. Rule 9.2.6 teaches “to nawi sa bifo no dili mesun” for “I eat twice a day.” Rule 8.2.1 and Lessons 9–10 give “bifo sa” for “twice,” following the regular noun-then-count order of 5.2.5. Change the rate example to “to nawi bifo sa no dili mesun,” or document a distinct rate construction if the reversal is intentional.

18. low — B2, B10. Lesson 1 explicitly pronounces “poka” (dog) as “TO-tu,” contradicting both lexicon.tsv and the one-letter-per-sound rule in 3.1. This is the worked example learners are asked to imitate. Change it to “PO-ka.” The lesson introduction also reports 22 loans, while loans.tsv contains 82. Update the inventory figures from the same edition as the lessons.

Verdict

Defer the form freeze. The “tiwu ye / tiwuye” pair already loses a written distinction in careful speech, and “ban kuwi” assigns two destinations the same conventional name. Both are cheaper to repair now. Settle the name boundary and affected-subject rules before teaching them. Real-listener error rates and child acquisition remain unverified; rationale.md §11 reports that one human read the language aloud and none listened.
